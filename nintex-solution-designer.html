In late 2024, every AI app generator was racing toward the same flashy demo: type what you want, get an instant app. It looked impressive. It didn't work. I led the design of Nintex's Solution Designer by going in the opposite direction, advocating for a methodical, multi-step approach when the entire market was obsessed with "vibe-coding" and one-shot generation.

This wasn't a safe bet. I repeatedly had to defend this vision to product leadership while competitors were getting viral traction with their instant-gratification experiences. But research with low-code professionals revealed what the hype missed: users need control at each stage, not hope that AI guesses right.

The risk paid off. Within months, major competitors began pivoting away from one-shot generation toward the exact multi-step, spec-driven patterns we had pioneered. We had essentially invented a new category: structured AI-assisted development for non-technical users.

The impact went beyond one feature. Solution Designer became Nintex's highest-value strategic initiative, designed to compress proof-of-concept timelines from weeks to hours for mid-market businesses, and the AI-assisted design patterns I established were adopted across multiple product teams.

01 — Context

Nintex already had all the pieces: workflow automation, web app builders, forms, document generation, and data connectors. Mid-sized businesses were looking to consolidate their software spend. Generative AI had just made it plausible, for the first time, that non-technical users could actually build production-ready software themselves, without expensive development teams or months of waiting.

In November 2024, I was invited as AI Design Lead to a week-long strategy workshop with senior product and engineering leadership in South Africa. Out of that workshop, one initiative emerged as the highest-value thing Nintex could build: an AI-powered Solution Designer that would serve as the gateway to the entire platform, letting customers experiment with all Nintex capabilities before committing to a higher-tier subscription.

The industry had been talking for decades about enabling non-technical people to build real software. This felt like the moment it was actually becoming possible.

The strategic stakes were high. Get it right and Nintex accelerates digital transformation for thousands of mid-market businesses. Get it wrong and the company burns credibility on an expensive feature that doesn't actually solve the problem it promised to solve.

02 — Research

The company had a pattern of starting with quick technical POCs and productionising features before proper research. With engineers leading the proof-of-concept phase, I knew the loudest technical voices would shape the solution, unless I could anchor decisions in real user evidence.

I quickly partnered with customer success and services teams to recruit external low-code/no-code builders, professionals who do this work daily for clients. Despite spending my career in software development, I knew LCNC development has its own dynamics. I needed to understand them properly before designing anything.

Finding 01
Iterations are shorter and cheaper

The cost of being wrong in LCNC is significantly lower than traditional development. Builders iterate fast and often, which changes what "good enough" requirements look like upfront.

Finding 02
Requirements emerge during building

Unlike traditional software projects, LCNC practitioners don't clarify all requirements before starting. Quick proofs of concept are used to surface requirements that couldn't have been articulated beforehand.

Finding 03
AI lowers the iteration cost further

Generating assets instead of building them manually makes the cost of being wrong even lower, which means the opportunity for iterative, guided generation is even more valuable than in traditional LCNC.

This research fundamentally changed the design direction. If LCNC users iterate quickly and discover requirements as they build, the AI experience couldn't be a one-shot black box. Users needed to guide the system, reviewing, adjusting, and steering, not just submitting prompts and hoping for the best.

03 — The Design Bet

The competitive landscape in early 2025 was dominated by one thing: instant gratification. One-shot generation demos were going viral. Vibe-coding was becoming a cultural moment. Products barely capable of generating prototypes were being marketed as the future of software development, and those experiences were setting expectations for everyone, including our product leadership.

The path of least resistance was obvious: build the same thing. A slick prompt box, an animation, an app appears. Ship it fast. Ride the wave. I argued for a different approach, directly, repeatedly, and with full awareness that I was going against the grain of what leadership was seeing in the market.

The strategic choice
What the market was doing

One-shot generation. Describe your app, get your app. Impressive demos, prototype-quality outputs, limited ability to steer or correct. Instant, but unreliable.

What I designed instead

Spec-driven development for non-technical users. Multi-step journey. Review outlines before generating details. Meaningful control at each stage. Slower to start, far more accurate in the end.

Asset Outlines

Central to the approach was the concept of asset specifications, AI-generated skeletons that users review before the system generates full detail. The logic is simple: wrong assumptions and hallucinations are far easier to catch in a skeleton than in a fully fleshed-out application. Catching them early means fewer iterations, less frustration, and more accurate final outputs.

Getting buy-in for this concept wasn't straightforward. The value wasn't obvious to all stakeholders, the outline lived in a separate workspace from the final asset (creating temporary confusion for users), and integrating the concept across 7 different product domains required winning over teams with their own roadmaps. I had to trust the vision while building the evidence to justify it.

04 — Design Process

After weeks of conversations where everyone seemed to agree, I put pen to paper. Low-fidelity sketches exposed misalignment instantly, things that had sounded like consensus turned out to be different mental models. The low-fidelity nature of the artifacts made it safe for stakeholders to contribute their own ideas and feel ownership over the direction, which paid dividends in buy-in later.

Because this initiative would shape the design-time AI architecture across all of Nintex's product domains, I made sure early designs reached leaders who weren't directly involved in the project but would be affected by its decisions. Winning their alignment early was critical , we needed their roadmaps to move with ours.

05 — Challenges
Establishing research before the POC ran away

The company's instinct in fast-moving spaces was to start with a technical proof-of-concept and figure out the design layer later. With engineers already leading the POC phase and generative AI moving quickly, there was a real risk that the solution's shape would be locked in before any user research had taken place, leaving design to decorate an already-made decision.

The response was to move fast on research rather than waiting for the POC to settle. By partnering with customer success teams to recruit external LCNC builders early, I could put user evidence on the table before the technical architecture calcified.

Selling a slower-feeling approach to a leadership team watching fast-moving competitors

The harder challenge wasn't the design itself, it was the sustained advocacy required to keep the approach alive. Product leadership was watching competitors ship viral demos. The market narrative was loud. A multi-step, spec-driven experience doesn't demo as cleanly as a single prompt box producing an app in seconds, and that gap in first impressions created real pressure.

Getting buy-in required building the case incrementally rather than winning a single argument. Presenting competitive analysis that showed where more mature players were heading, framing the research findings explicitly against the one-shot failure modes, and using low-fidelity sketches to make the concept tangible enough to evaluate, each of these created a stronger foundation for the direction without requiring stakeholders to simply take it on faith.

The outline concept and the two-workspace problem

The asset outline, a generated skeleton that users review before the system fills in full detail, was central to the design's value. But it introduced a UX problem: the outline lived in a different workspace from the final asset. Users had to mentally bridge two contexts, which created confusion that wasn't obvious until we put the design in front of people.

What it required was ongoing refinement of how the two workspaces related to each other, making the connection legible without forcing users to context-switch unnecessarily. The value of catching wrong assumptions early had to outweigh the cost of the added step, and making that trade-off feel worthwhile to users was an active design problem throughout.

Scaling one concept across seven product domains

The design patterns developed for Solution Designer needed to work across workflows, web apps, forms, processes, document packages, data tables, and connectors, seven domains, each with its own product team, its own roadmap, and its own view of what the AI experience should look like for their users.

The approach was to run a design workshop that established a shared framework before teams went off to implement independently. Getting teams to adopt patterns they didn't originate required making the framework flexible enough to accommodate domain-specific needs while being opinionated enough to hold together as a system. Early alignment on principles, rather than prescribing exact UI, gave teams room to adapt without drifting.

06 — Validation

Months after we had committed to the spec-driven approach, major competitors moved in the same direction. The industry shifted from one-shot generation and conversational editing toward multi-step generation journeys with contextual AI interactions for precise refinement, closer to what we had already built.

This wasn't prescience, it was the result of making decisions based on research rather than market noise. When the loudest voices were pointing one way, having user evidence gave us a more stable foundation to stand on.

At a staff level, that's part of the job: knowing when to hold a position under pressure, and knowing why. The market eventually catching up is less important than making the right call for users at the time.
